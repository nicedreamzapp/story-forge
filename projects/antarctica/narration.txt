At the bottom of the world there is a continent we have known for two hundred years, and never once seen.
Antarctica wears a sheet of ice up to four kilometers thick, holding most of the fresh water on Earth.
For generations, our maps of the land beneath it were mostly guesswork.
Then, in March of twenty twenty-five, scientists finished a project called Bedmap three.
Eighty-two million measurements, from planes, ships, satellites, and even dog sleds, stitched into one map.
Strip the ice away, and this is what has been hiding under our feet the whole time.
Mountain ranges no human eye has ever seen.
And in Wilkes Land, a canyon buried under more ice than almost anywhere on the planet, nearly five kilometers deep.
We had better maps of Mars.

[scene2]
The ice is not a lid on a dead world.
In twenty twenty-five, satellites found eighty-five new lakes hiding beneath the ice sheet, joining hundreds already known.
Whole networks of them, filling and draining in slow heartbeats, connected by rivers that have never seen the sky.
And the water is alive.
When scientists drilled into one of these buried lakes, they found microbes thriving in the dark, feeding on iron and sulfur instead of sunlight.
Then came the accident.
In twenty twenty-one, a drill team punched through nine hundred meters of the Filchner-Ronne ice shelf, aiming for plain mud.
Their camera landed on a boulder, two hundred sixty kilometers from open ocean, in water below freezing, in total darkness.
The boulder was covered in life.
Sponges, and creatures nobody had ever named, holding on in a place where life was not supposed to be possible.

[scene3]
The rock itself remembers a different Antarctica.
In twenty twenty, a seabed core near Pine Island Glacier came up holding the roots of a rainforest.
Ninety million years ago, tree ferns and conifers grew here, in a climate as mild as Seattle.
There are mountains under the ice as large as the Alps, the Gamburtsevs, born half a billion years ago.
The ice that buried them also protected them, so their peaks are still sharp, frozen in time.
Radar has even traced the ghosts of ancient rivers, valleys that once carried water toward Australia, back when the continents were one.
And the ice remembers too.
In January of twenty twenty-five, a drill at Little Dome C brought up a core almost three kilometers long.
Inside it, trapped bubble by bubble, is one-point-two million years of the planet's atmosphere, the oldest unbroken climate record we have ever held.

[scene4]
But the continent we just met is already changing.
On the far western edge sits Thwaites, a glacier the size of Florida, holding back enough ice to raise the seas by more than half a meter.
In twenty twenty, scientists melted a hole seven hundred meters deep, and sent a robot named Icefin down into the dark.
It swam to the grounding line, the hidden place where the glacier first lifts off the bedrock, and filmed it for the first time in history.
What it found was a surprise.
The ice is not simply melting away, it is fracturing, as warm water carves staircases and crevasses into its base.
In twenty years, the grounding line has retreated fourteen kilometers.
Thwaites is speaking, and now, finally, we know how to listen.
Two centuries after we found Antarctica, we have only just met it.
A continent with buried mountains, secret lakes, forests in its stones, and a memory over a million years long.
The age of exploration never ended.
It just went under the ice.
