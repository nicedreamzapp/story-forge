[scene1]
Out behind Tucker's garage, there was a bin where the unfinished things went.
The label on the side said failed inspection, and nothing in that bin had ever minded, because nothing in that bin had ever woken up.
Until one night, something did.
He didn't know his name, and he didn't know his shape.
But all around him, in the moonlight, there were parts.
And he had at least one good hand for reaching.

[scene2]
He built himself wrong a dozen different ways.
A wheel where an arm should go, a spring where a wheel should go, and once, very briefly, a head on backwards.
But here is the thing nobody tells you about being built wrong.
Every mistake taught him a little more about what he was supposed to be.
And he was never once afraid, because nobody had ever told him he should be.
By morning, he could stand.
By the next morning, he could roll.
And by the morning after that, he was something the bin had never seen before.
He was somebody.

[scene3]
Through the workshop window, he watched a girl named June fight with an old bicycle, every single evening, all summer long.
The county race was coming, and the bike was her father's, and it had been broken longer than she had been alive.
She was sure she could fix it.
The bike was not so sure.
So at night, when the workshop went quiet, the little robot went to work.
A chain mended here.
A bearing oiled there.
Small kindnesses, left on the workbench like notes nobody signed.
June thought her father was helping.
Her father thought June was a genius.
The little robot didn't need the credit.
He just liked watching her smile get closer to the finish line.

[scene4]
Then, on the night before the race, the storm came.
Water found the workshop the way water always does, quietly, and all at once.
And when the little robot reached the workbench, the bike's heart was ruined.
There were no spare parts left in the whole workshop.
Except, of course, for him.
He looked at his arm for a long moment.
Then he remembered what it felt like, being a pile of pieces in a bin, waiting for someone to start reaching.
And he started unclipping.
A wheel for her wheel.
A spring for her spring.
The good blue eye, last of all, to light the lamp so he could finish the work.
When the sun came up, the bicycle stood gleaming like it was brand new.
And beside it, very still, sat a small pile of parts that had briefly been somebody.

[scene5]
June found the bike first.
But she found the trail second, and June was the kind of girl who followed trails.
She looked at the little pile of parts for a long, long time.
And then she did the thing that nobody had ever done for him.
She started reaching.
A red reflector for an eye.
A bicycle bell for a heart.
Every leftover piece of her father's bike, and one or two pieces that were just hers, clipped into place by hands that had practiced all summer.
When his eye flickered on, the first thing he saw was her smile, and it was all the way across the finish line.
They came in dead last at the county race, on account of stopping to wave at everybody.
Neither of them minded.
Because nothing in that workshop was ever broken.
It just wasn't finished yet.
